Telling someone they did not get the job is one of the least pleasant parts of hiring, so a lot of people avoid it. They ghost. They send a cold one-line template. They wait three weeks and then forget entirely. Every one of those choices costs your company something, because the people you reject talk, apply again, and sometimes become your customers. This guide shows you how to write a rejection email that is clear, kind, and quick to send, without making promises you cannot keep.

## Why every candidate deserves a reply

A candidate gave you their time. They tailored a resume, blocked out an interview slot, and probably rehearsed answers the night before. The least you owe them is a clear answer instead of silence.

Silence is not neutral. To the person waiting, it reads as rejection plus disrespect. They will remember it, mention it to friends, and post about it. Your employer brand is built one candidate experience at a time, and the rejected far outnumber the hired.

There is a practical payoff too. The person you pass on today might be the perfect fit for a different role next quarter, or a referral source for someone who is. A graceful no keeps that door open. A cold one slams it.

## The structure of a good rejection email

Every effective rejection follows roughly the same shape. You can adapt the warmth and detail to the situation, but the bones stay the same.

1. **Thank them, specifically.** Name the role and acknowledge their time.
2. **Deliver the decision clearly and early.** Do not bury it under three paragraphs of cushioning.
3. **Add a little genuine warmth.** One sincere line about something positive.
4. **Offer feedback only if appropriate.** More on the limits of this below.
5. **Keep the door open, but only when you mean it.** No false hope.
6. **Close politely with a real signature.**

The goal is to be unmistakable without being harsh. A candidate should never have to read your email twice to figure out whether they are still in the running.

## Lead with the decision, kindly

The single most common mistake is making people hunt for the answer. Do not open with a paragraph of praise that makes them think an offer is coming, only to pivot with a "however." That whiplash feels worse than a plain no.

State the outcome in the first or second sentence, wrapped in a thank-you. Something like, "Thank you for taking the time to interview for the role. After careful consideration, we have decided to move forward with another candidate." Clear, complete, and respectful.

The same applies to the subject line. Use the role name so the message is easy to find and recognize, for example "Update on your application for the Marketing Coordinator role." Avoid anything that hints at good news when there is none.

## How much feedback to give, and the legal caution

Candidates often want to know why. Real feedback is a generous gift, but it carries risk, so handle it deliberately.

For most rejections, especially early-stage ones, keep it brief and process-focused: you had a strong pool, another candidate was a closer fit for the current needs. For finalists who invested several rounds, a sentence or two of specific, honest feedback is a kindness worth offering.

A few rules keep feedback safe and useful:

- **Stick to job-related observations.** Skills, experience, and role fit only. Never reference anything tied to age, gender, race, religion, disability, or other protected characteristics.
- **Be specific but not exhaustive.** "We were looking for more hands-on experience with paid social" is helpful. A line-by-line teardown is not.
- **Offer, do not impose.** For finalists, it is often kinder to invite them to reach out for feedback than to dump it unprompted.
- **Check your local rules.** Employment law varies by country and region. When in doubt, your HR or legal team should set the policy on what feedback is shared.

If you are unsure whether a comment is safe to put in writing, leave it out. A vague-but-warm rejection beats a specific one that creates exposure.

## Tailor the message to the stage

A candidate who failed a five-minute screen and a finalist who sat through four interviews deserve different emails. The early-stage note can be short and template-driven. The final-round note should be personal, slower, and ideally preceded by a phone call for senior roles.

Here is an early-stage rejection after a first screen.

> Subject: Update on your application for the Marketing Coordinator role
>
> Hi Jordan,
>
> Thank you for applying to the Marketing Coordinator role and for speaking with me last week. We received a large number of strong applications, and after review we have decided to move forward with other candidates whose experience more closely matches what the role needs right now.
>
> I appreciate the time and care you put into your application. We will keep your details on file, and I would genuinely welcome you to apply again as new roles open up.
>
> Wishing you all the best in your search.
>
> Warm regards,
> Priya Nair
> Talent Team, Brightwave

Now a final-round rejection, where the candidate earned more warmth and a bit of feedback.

> Subject: Update on the Senior Designer role
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> Thank you for the time you gave us across all four conversations, and for the thoughtful portfolio walkthrough. This was a close decision, and I want to be straight with you: we have decided to extend an offer to another candidate.
>
> You impressed the whole panel, especially with how you talked through your design process. The deciding factor came down to depth of experience leading a team, which the role leans on heavily. That is the only gap we saw, and it is one a couple more years will close.
>
> I would be glad to keep in touch as your career grows, and I mean that. If you would find more detailed feedback useful, just reply and I will set up a quick call.
>
> Thank you again, and best of luck.
>
> Best,
> Priya Nair
> Talent Team, Brightwave

The second email is longer because the relationship earned it. The first is brief because dragging it out would feel hollow.

## Timing matters as much as wording

A kind email sent a month late still feels like neglect. Once a decision is final, send the rejection within a few business days. For candidates you interviewed in person, do not let them sit through a weekend wondering.

If your process is still open and you are keeping a backup pool, it is fine to tell people honestly that a decision is taking longer, rather than rejecting prematurely. What you should never do is leave anyone in indefinite silence. Even a short "we are still deciding and will update you by Friday" beats nothing.

Aim to treat the rejection with the same care you would a welcome message to a new hire. The principles overlap more than you might think, and our guide to writing a [welcome email](/blog/welcome-email) shows how much a thoughtful first or last impression shapes the relationship.

## What to avoid

A few habits quietly damage your reputation. Steer clear of these.

- **False hope.** Do not say "we will definitely keep you in mind" unless you actually will. Candidates remember broken promises.
- **Canned coldness.** A pure form-letter with no name and no warmth reads as contempt. A small personal touch costs you fifteen seconds.
- **Over-explaining.** You do not owe a full justification, and the more you write, the more you risk saying something you cannot defend.
- **Blaming the candidate.** Frame it as fit and timing, not as their failure.

The same calm, human tone that works in good customer support works here too. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I tell a candidate exactly why they were rejected?

For finalists, a sentence or two of honest, job-related feedback is a genuine kindness, and many appreciate it deeply. For earlier-stage candidates, a brief explanation about fit and a strong applicant pool is usually enough. Always keep feedback tied to skills and role requirements, never to anything that could be a protected characteristic. If you are unsure whether a comment is safe to put in writing, leave it out or offer a call instead.

### Is it okay to use a template for rejection emails?

Yes, templates are practical and even expected at volume, but they should be starting points, not the whole message. Personalize at least the candidate's name and the role, and add one specific line for anyone you interviewed. A template that reads as a cold form letter does more harm than no template at all. The professional, clear structure in our guide to [how to write a professional email](/blog/how-to-write-a-professional-email) applies directly here.

### How soon after a decision should I send the rejection?

Within a few business days of the decision being final. For candidates who interviewed in person or made it to later rounds, faster is better, ideally before a weekend so they are not left waiting. If your process is still genuinely open, it is more honest to tell people there is a delay than to reject them early or leave them in silence.

## The lasting impression

A rejection email is the last thing a candidate hears from you, and it is what they will remember. Make it clear, send it promptly, add real warmth, and never promise what you will not deliver. Treat the people you turn away as well as the ones you hire, and your reputation will quietly compound in your favor.
