There is a line of text in your email that shows up right next to the subject in almost every inbox, and most senders never write it on purpose. It is called the preheader, and when ignored it gets filled with whatever junk sits at the top of your email. This guide explains what preheader text is, why a blank or default one wastes prime real estate, and exactly how to write one that earns the open.

## What Preheader Text Actually Is

The preheader is the preview snippet that appears alongside or beneath your subject line in the inbox, before anyone opens the email. On a phone it might be a single line under the subject. In a desktop client it often trails after the subject on the same row.

It is pulled from the first readable text in your email's body. That is the crucial part. If you do not set it deliberately, the inbox grabs the first text it finds, and that is rarely what you want it to say.

Think of the subject and preheader as a pair working together. The subject is the headline. The preheader is the subhead that decides whether the headline pays off. Together they are most of what a reader uses to decide whether to open.

## Why a Blank or Default Preheader Hurts You

When you skip the preheader, the inbox fills the gap automatically, and the results are almost always bad. You have all seen them.

- **"View this email in your browser."** The single most common wasted preheader, because that link often sits at the very top of the template.
- **"Is this email not displaying correctly?"** Another stock line that screams template rather than message.
- **An image alt text or a stray "img" reference**, which looks broken and unprofessional.
- **A repeat of the subject line**, which adds nothing and tells the reader there is no new information here.

Every one of those is a missed chance. You get a free line of text in the most competitive space in the inbox, and you are spending it on plumbing. A deliberate preheader turns that wasted line into a second reason to open.

## How to Write a Preheader That Works

The job of the preheader is simple: extend the subject line and give one more reason to open. A few principles get you most of the way.

1. **Complement the subject, do not repeat it.** If the subject raises a question, the preheader can hint at the answer. If the subject names the topic, the preheader adds the benefit.
2. **Add value or curiosity.** Tell the reader what is inside, or open a small loop they will want closed.
3. **Front-load the important words.** Like subject lines, preheaders get cut off, so the meaning should land in the first several words.
4. **Match the contents.** A preheader that overpromises gets the open but loses the trust, and that costs you on the next send.

The subject and preheader should read like one continuous thought. When they do, the pair feels intentional, and intentional emails get opened.

## Mind the Length and How It Truncates

Preheader length is not fixed, because every email client shows a different amount. This is the part that trips people up.

- A mobile inbox might show roughly 35 to 50 characters of preheader.
- A desktop client might show 100 characters or more, sometimes much more.
- Some clients show very little; others spill the preheader well into the body text.

You cannot control where each client cuts off, so design for the short case and let the long case be a bonus. Aim to land your core message in the **first 40 to 50 characters**, then let a bit more trail off for clients that show it. A preheader that only makes sense once you read all 130 characters will fall flat on the phone, where most opens happen.

One more detail: do not pad the preheader to fill space, and do not leave the rest of the line to be filled by random body text. The spacer trick below solves that cleanly.

## The Preheader Spacer Trick

Here is the practical problem. You write a great 45-character preheader, but the client shows 100 characters, so it grabs the next 55 characters straight from your email body. Now your polished preview is followed by "Hi Jordan, welcome to" or some half-sentence that makes no sense.

The fix is a **spacer**: a stretch of invisible characters placed right after your preheader text, before the visible body begins. It pushes the leftover body text out of the preview, so the inbox shows your preheader and then trails off into nothing instead of into a fragment.

Most modern email platforms make this easy. They give you a dedicated preheader field, and they handle the spacing for you. If yours does, use that field and you are done. If you are hand-coding, you add a hidden text block at the very top of the body, styled so it does not display in the open email but still feeds the preview. Either way, the goal is the same: your chosen words, then clean empty space, not a random sentence fragment.

## Weak vs. Strong Pairs

The best way to feel the difference is to compare the subject and preheader as a unit. Here are a few realistic pairs for a small garden supply shop.

A weak pair, where the preheader repeats or wastes space:

> Subject: Our spring newsletter is here
>
> Preheader: View this email in your browser

That gives the reader nothing new. The subject is vague, and the preheader is plumbing.

A strong pair, where the two lines build on each other:

> Subject: 3 vegetables to plant before the last frost
>
> Preheader: Plus the soil mix that doubled our tomato yield

Now the preheader adds a second hook. The reader gets two reasons to open instead of one, and neither line is wasted.

Here is another, for a product announcement:

> Subject: Your new scheduling dashboard is live
>
> Preheader: See both store schedules on one screen in under a minute

The subject states the news; the preheader states the payoff. Together they answer "what is this and why should I care" before the reader even opens.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the preheader the same as the subject line?

No. The subject line is the bold headline shown first, and the preheader is the smaller preview text shown next to or beneath it. They appear together in the inbox before anyone opens the email. The mistake is treating them as the same thing and either repeating the subject in the preheader or ignoring the preheader entirely. Used well, they are a headline and a subhead that work as a team.

### What happens if I do not set a preheader at all?

The inbox fills it for you, pulling the first readable text from your email body. That usually means a "view in browser" link, a "not displaying correctly" line, or some stray fragment, none of which help you. You lose a free line of valuable preview space and your email looks less polished. Setting the preheader deliberately is one of the lowest-effort, highest-return tweaks in email.

### How long should preheader text be?

Put your core message in the first 40 to 50 characters, since that is roughly what a mobile inbox shows, then let a bit more trail off for clients that display more. Avoid writing something that only makes sense at full length, because most opens happen on phones where it gets cut. If your platform offers a preheader field, use it and add a spacer so leftover body text does not leak into the preview.

## A Small Line With Big Leverage

The preheader is one short line, but it sits in the most valuable space you have: the gap between sending an email and getting it opened. Stop letting the inbox fill it with "view in browser." Write it on purpose, make it complement the subject, keep the meaning up front, and use a spacer so it ends cleanly. It takes a minute and it works on every email you send, which makes it one of the easiest upgrades in your whole program.
