Most of us write dozens of emails a day without thinking much about them, which is exactly how small habits turn into reputation problems. A vague subject line, a missing attachment, or a tone that reads colder than you meant can quietly cost you replies, deals, and trust. This guide walks through 15 common email mistakes and shows you the simple fix for each, with real examples you can copy.

## Mistakes That Hurt Your First Impression

The opening seconds of an email decide whether it gets read, skimmed, or ignored. These four mistakes do the most damage before your recipient even reaches your main point.

### 1. Writing a vague or missing subject line

A subject line like "Quick question" or "Hi" tells the reader nothing and often lands in the "later" pile that never gets opened. A good subject line is specific and scannable, so the recipient knows the topic and any action needed.

> Weak: Following up
>
> Better: Following up on the Q3 budget review (due Friday)

Aim for clarity over cleverness. If there is a deadline or a decision required, put it right in the subject.

### 2. Using the wrong greeting (or none at all)

Skipping a greeting can read as abrupt, while an overly stiff one can feel forced. Match the greeting to the relationship. "Hi Sarah," works for most professional exchanges. Reserve "Dear Ms. Chen," for formal first contact, and avoid "Hey" with people you do not know well.

One thing many people get wrong: spelling the recipient's name incorrectly. Double-check it against their signature or email address before you hit send. Few things undercut a polished message faster than "Dear Micheal" when his name is Michael.

### 3. Burying the point

Many readers skim, especially on a phone. If your main request is in paragraph four, it may never get seen. State your purpose in the first sentence or two, then add context below.

> Buried: Hope you had a great weekend. I was thinking about the project and the timeline and a few other things, and after some reflection I wanted to reach out because...
>
> Direct: Could you approve the attached invoice by Thursday? Details below.

### 4. Forgetting the attachment

The classic mistake. You write "Please see the attached file," then send without attaching anything. A simple habit fixes it: attach the file first, before you write a single word. If you mention an attachment, your email client may even warn you when one is missing, so do not ignore that prompt.

## Mistakes With Tone and Wording

Email strips out tone of voice and facial expression, so words carry more weight than they would in person. These mistakes are the ones most likely to be misread.

### 5. Sounding colder than you intend

Short, efficient emails can read as curt even when you mean nothing by it. A single line like "Send it over" can feel like a command. You do not need to pad your message, but a small softening word or a thank-you goes a long way.

> Curt: Need this by 5.
>
> Warmer: Could you get this to me by 5 today? Thanks so much.

### 6. Overusing exclamation points and emoji

A little warmth helps, but a message with five exclamation points can read as anxious or unprofessional. Use one where it genuinely fits, and let your words do the rest. The same goes for emoji: fine with close colleagues, risky with new clients or senior leadership.

### 7. Writing when you are angry

If an email makes your blood pressure spike, do not reply right away. Draft your response, leave it in your drafts folder, and reread it an hour later. You will almost always soften it, and you will never regret the version you did not send in anger. A useful test: would you be comfortable if this email were forwarded to your boss or read aloud in a meeting?

### 8. Being passive-aggressive

Phrases like "Per my last email," "As I mentioned previously," or "Just circling back again" signal frustration even when you are trying to sound neutral. If you genuinely need to repeat yourself, do it plainly and kindly.

> Passive-aggressive: Per my last email, this was due Monday.
>
> Clear and kind: Following up on my note from Monday. Are you still able to send this over today?

## Mistakes in Structure and Formatting

How an email looks affects how easily it gets read and acted on. These mistakes make your message harder to process than it needs to be.

### 9. Sending a wall of text

A dense block of text is exhausting on screen. Break your message into short paragraphs, and use a bulleted or numbered list whenever you have multiple points or steps. Compare these two versions of the same request:

- What you need
- Why it matters
- When you need it by

That structure lets a busy reader absorb everything in a glance, instead of mining a paragraph for the parts that apply to them.

### 10. Asking for too many things at once

If your email contains five separate questions, expect the reader to answer one and miss the rest. When you have several asks, number them so each one is visible and easy to respond to. For anything truly complex, consider whether a quick call would be faster than a long thread.

### 11. Neglecting the closing and signature

Ending abruptly after your last sentence feels unfinished. A simple sign-off ("Best," "Thanks," "Kind regards,") plus a clear signature gives the reader your name, role, and how to reach you. An incomplete signature also makes you harder to contact and easier to forget. ## Mistakes With Recipients and Sending

Some of the most damaging email mistakes have nothing to do with your writing and everything to do with where the message goes.

### 12. Reply All when you mean Reply

Reply All has its place, but firing a "Sounds good!" to forty people clutters inboxes and can broadcast something you meant for one person. Before you send, glance at the recipient list and ask whether everyone on it truly needs your reply.

### 13. Mishandling CC and BCC

CC keeps people informed; BCC hides recipients from each other. Two common errors:

1. CC'ing someone's manager to apply pressure, which reads as escalation and damages trust.
2. Sending to a large external list in the To or CC field, exposing everyone's address. Use BCC for group announcements so you do not leak contacts.

### 14. Sending to the wrong person

Autocomplete is convenient and dangerous. Type "Dan" and your client may pick the wrong Dan entirely. Verify the address before sending anything sensitive, and slow down on confidential messages. Some email clients let you set a short send delay, which gives you a window to catch a mistake before it leaves your outbox.

### 15. Skipping the proofread

Typos, autocorrect surprises, and half-finished sentences make even strong work look careless. Reread your email once before sending, ideally out loud for anything important. Pay special attention to names, numbers, dates, and the very first line, since those are the details people notice most. A thirty-second read can save you an embarrassing correction email later.

## A Quick Pre-Send Checklist

Before you hit send, run through these five questions:

- Is the subject line specific and clear?
- Did I lead with my main point or request?
- Are the attachments actually attached?
- Is the recipient list correct, with CC and BCC used properly?
- Did I reread it for tone and typos?

Make this a habit and most of the mistakes above disappear on their own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a professional email be?

As short as it can be while still being clear and polite. Most everyday emails work best at a few short paragraphs. If you find yourself writing past two or three hundred words, ask whether a numbered list, a shared document, or a quick call would serve the reader better.

### Is it rude to send a follow-up email?

Not at all, as long as you give it reasonable time and keep the tone friendly. Waiting a few business days is usually fair. Reference your original message briefly, restate the request, and avoid language that sounds accusatory. People are busy, and a gentle nudge is often welcome rather than annoying.

### Should I use my personal email for work?

Generally no. A work or custom-domain address looks more professional and keeps your personal and professional lives separate. If you are a freelancer or small-business owner without a company domain, a clean, consistent signature still helps your emails look trustworthy and makes you easy to contact.

Email is the most common way we represent ourselves at work, and small improvements compound fast. You do not need to fix all 15 habits overnight. Pick the two or three that you recognize in your own inbox, practice them this week, and the rest will start to feel natural. Clear, considerate email is a skill, and like any skill, it gets easier every time you use it.
