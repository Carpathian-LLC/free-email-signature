Professors get a lot of email, and the ones that are clear, polite, and easy to answer get the fastest replies. A vague message signed "sent from my phone" gets pushed to the bottom of the pile. The good news is that a good academic email follows a simple, repeatable format. This guide walks you through that format and gives you ready-to-use templates for the five requests students send most.

## Start with the right basics

Before you write a word of the body, get three things right. They matter more than you think.

First, **use your school email address**, not a personal account with a nickname. A message from a recognizable university address lands in the inbox; a random Gmail can get filtered or ignored. If you have not set up a clean academic address yet, our guide to choosing a [professional email address](/blog/professional-email-address) explains what to look for.

Second, **write a clear subject line** that names the course and the topic. "ENGL 210: Question about Friday's essay deadline" tells the professor exactly what they are opening and lets them find it later. Avoid "Hi" or "Quick question," which tell them nothing.

Third, **remember that professors teach many students** across several courses. They cannot place you from your first name alone. That is why the body has to identify you clearly, which we will cover next.

## The structure of a good email to a professor

Almost every email to a professor follows the same shape. Keep it short and they will thank you for it.

1. **A proper greeting** using their correct title and last name.
2. **One sentence saying who you are**: your name and the specific course and section.
3. **One clear request or question.** Just one per email when you can.
4. **Any necessary detail**, kept brief.
5. **A polite close** and a simple signature with your full name and student details.

The whole thing should usually fit on one screen. A professor reading on a phone between classes should be able to grasp it in fifteen seconds and reply just as fast.

## Get the greeting right

The opening line sets the tone, and students get it wrong more than any other part. Default to formal until told otherwise.

- Use **"Dear Professor Lastname"** or **"Dear Dr. Lastname."** Both are safe.
- Use "Dr." only if they hold a doctorate; "Professor" is a safe catch-all for faculty.
- Avoid "Hey," "Hi [first name]," "Mr./Mrs.," or "Miss" unless your professor has explicitly invited that.
- When you do not know their preference, "Professor Lastname" is almost never wrong.

If you want a deeper look at openings for any formal email, our guide on [how to start an email](/blog/how-to-start-an-email) covers the greetings that build instant credibility. Getting this first line right signals that you take the relationship seriously.

## Five templates for common requests

Here are the messages students send most often, written the way a professor wants to receive them.

### Missing a class

> Subject: BIO 140 (Section 2): Missing Thursday's lecture
>
> Dear Professor Adeyemi,
>
> My name is Lena Park, a student in your BIO 140 Section 2. I am writing to let you know I will miss Thursday's lecture because of a medical appointment I could not reschedule.
>
> I will review the posted slides and get notes from a classmate. Is there anything from that session I should be sure not to miss, such as an in-class activity?
>
> Thank you,
> Lena Park
> Student ID 88421, BIO 140 Section 2

### Asking for an extension

> Subject: HIST 305: Request for a short extension on the essay
>
> Dear Dr. Okafor,
>
> My name is Marcus Lee, in your HIST 305 course. I am writing to ask whether a two-day extension on the research essay due Friday might be possible. I have had two major assignments land in the same week and want to give your essay the attention it deserves rather than rush it.
>
> I understand if this is not possible and will submit on time if needed. Thank you for considering it.
>
> Best regards,
> Marcus Lee
> HIST 305, Tuesday/Thursday section

Ask early, give a brief honest reason, and never demand. An extension is a favor, not a right.

### Requesting a meeting

> Subject: PSY 220: Request to meet during office hours
>
> Dear Professor Singh,
>
> My name is Aisha Rahman, a student in your PSY 220 lecture. I would like to come to your office hours this week to talk through the research methods section, which I am finding tricky.
>
> Are your Wednesday hours from 2 to 4 still open? If another time works better, I am flexible. Thank you.
>
> Best,
> Aisha Rahman
> PSY 220, Monday/Wednesday section

If your professor lists office hours, use them first and mention them by name. It shows you read the syllabus.

### Asking about a grade

> Subject: CHEM 101: Question about my midterm grade
>
> Dear Dr. Voss,
>
> My name is Daniel Cho, in your CHEM 101 course. Thank you for grading the midterms so quickly. I had a question about question 7 on mine and would like to understand where I lost points, so I can do better next time.
>
> Could I stop by office hours to go through it with you, or would you prefer I email the specific question? Thank you for your time.
>
> Best regards,
> Daniel Cho
> CHEM 101, Section 4

Frame a grade question as wanting to learn, not to argue. Ask to understand, not to demand points back.

### Requesting a recommendation

> Subject: Request for a recommendation letter (former student, ENGL 210)
>
> Dear Professor Bauer,
>
> My name is Sofia Alvarez. I took your ENGL 210 course last spring and earned an A on the final portfolio. I am applying to a summer research program and am hoping you might be willing to write me a letter of recommendation.
>
> The deadline is March 15, so there is plenty of time. If you are able to, I will send my resume, the program details, and a reminder of my work in your class to make it easier. Thank you so much for considering it.
>
> Warm regards,
> Sofia Alvarez

Always ask well ahead of the deadline, remind them how they know you, and offer to provide materials that make their job easier.

## Be concise, polite, and proofread

A professor's time is limited, so respect it. State your request in the first two or three sentences and resist the urge to over-explain. If your email is more than a short screen long, ask yourself what you can cut.

Proofread before you send. Typos, missing capitalization, and text-speak make you look careless, and they make your request easier to deprioritize. Read it once out loud; you will catch most mistakes. The same habits that make any message effective apply here, and our guide on [how to write a professional email](/blog/how-to-write-a-professional-email) is worth a read for the broader skill.

A polite "thank you" costs nothing and helps a lot. So does patience with the reply, which we will cover next.

## Response times and a simple signature

Professors are not on call. Give them at least two to three business days before following up, and do not email at midnight expecting a morning answer. If your request is genuinely urgent, say so politely and explain why, but use that sparingly.

When you do follow up, keep it brief and reference your original message rather than resending the whole thing. One gentle nudge after a few days is reasonable; a daily stream is not.

Close with a simple, clean signature. You do not need anything fancy. Your full name, your student ID if relevant, and the course identify you instantly. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How should I address a professor if I do not know whether they have a doctorate?

Use "Professor Lastname." It is a safe, respectful catch-all for faculty and almost never causes offense. "Dr. Lastname" is also fine if you know they hold a doctorate, but if you are unsure, "Professor" avoids the risk of getting it wrong. Avoid "Mr.," "Mrs.," or "Miss," which can read as dismissive in an academic setting, and skip first names until the professor invites them.

### How long should I wait before following up?

Give your professor at least two to three business days before sending a polite follow-up, and remember that weekends and breaks slow things down. When you do follow up, keep it short, friendly, and reference your original email by subject or date rather than resending the entire message. One reminder is reasonable; repeated daily emails are not. If something is truly time-sensitive, say so clearly and explain the deadline in your first message.

### Is it okay to email a professor about a grade?

Yes, as long as you frame it as wanting to understand rather than to argue. Thank them for grading, ask specifically where you lost points, and offer to discuss it in office hours so the conversation stays constructive. Professors are far more receptive to "help me learn from this" than to "give me my points back." Always stay polite, even if you disagree with the mark.

## The short version

Emailing a professor well is mostly about respect and clarity. Use your school address, write a specific subject line, greet them properly, say who you are and what course you are in, make one clear request, and proofread. Do that and you will get faster, friendlier replies all semester. Finish with a clean, simple signature so the professor always knows exactly who is writing.
