A good meeting request email respects the reader's time before it asks for any. The difference between a yes and a silent inbox usually comes down to a few small choices: a clear subject line, a specific reason, and an easy way to say yes. This guide walks you through exactly what to write, with real examples you can adapt for clients, coworkers, recruiters, or anyone whose calendar you want a piece of.

## What a meeting request email actually needs to do

Before you write a word, get clear on the job this email is doing. A meeting request is a small negotiation. You are asking someone to trade time they could spend on other things for time with you. Your email needs to make that trade feel obviously worthwhile and easy to accept.

Every effective meeting request answers four questions, usually in this order:

1. **Why are you reaching out?** The purpose, in one line.
2. **What is in it for them?** The value or outcome.
3. **How long will it take?** A specific duration.
4. **When can we do it?** Concrete options, not "let me know your availability."

If your draft is missing any of these, the recipient has to do extra work to respond, and extra work is the most common reason emails go unanswered.

## Write a subject line that earns the open

The subject line decides whether the rest of your effort gets read. Keep it short, specific, and oriented around the meeting itself. Vague lines like "Quick question" or "Touching base" tell the reader nothing and often get ignored.

Good subject lines name the topic and signal a small ask:

- Meeting request: Q3 budget review (20 min)
- 15 minutes to discuss the Henderson account?
- Intro call about your frontend role
- Following up: pricing for the spring campaign

Notice that several of these include the time commitment right in the subject. Telling someone up front that you only need 15 or 20 minutes lowers the perceived cost of saying yes.

## Structure the body so it reads in ten seconds

Most people skim email. Write so the key information survives a skim. That means short paragraphs, the purpose near the top, and the ask near the bottom where it is easy to find.

Here is a reliable structure:

- **Greeting** that uses their name.
- **One-line context** so they remember who you are or why this matters.
- **The reason** for the meeting, stated plainly.
- **The proposed details**: length, format (call, video, in person), and two or three time options.
- **A clear close** that makes responding easy.

### A complete example: requesting a meeting with a client

Say you are a freelancer who wants to walk a client through a project proposal. Here is how that comes together.

> **Subject:** Meeting request: reviewing your website proposal (30 min)
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> Thanks again for sending over the brief last week. I've put together a proposal for the website redesign and I'd love to walk you through it so you can ask questions in real time rather than over email.
>
> Would a 30 minute video call work? I'm free Tuesday the 10th at 2pm or Thursday the 12th anytime before noon, but I'm happy to work around your schedule if neither suits.
>
> Looking forward to it,
> Maria

This works because it opens with a small thank you, gives a clear reason ("walk you through it"), explains the benefit ("ask questions in real time"), names a duration, and offers specific times. Dana can reply with a single line.

## Give specific times, not open-ended questions

The single biggest improvement you can make to a meeting request is to stop asking "When are you free?" That question hands all the work to the recipient. They have to open their calendar, find gaps, guess what works for you, and propose options. Many people put that off, and "later" quietly becomes "never."

Instead, propose two or three concrete windows and let them pick:

> Would any of these work for a 20 minute call?
>
> - Wednesday at 10am
> - Wednesday at 3pm
> - Friday at 1pm
>
> If none of those fit, just send me a couple of times that do and I'll make one work.

This flips the dynamic. Now the reader is choosing from a short menu, which takes seconds, and you have a fallback that still keeps things moving. If you use a scheduling tool, you can offer a link as one option, but always pair it with a sentence in plain language so the email does not feel automated.

## Match your tone to the relationship

The same request reads differently depending on who is on the other end. A note to a close colleague can be casual. A first email to a senior executive or a potential client should be more measured and a little more formal. The structure stays the same, but the warmth and word choice shift.

### A cold or first-contact example

When you are emailing someone who does not know you, do more work to establish relevance and less to make small talk. Lead with why you are a worthwhile use of their time.

> **Subject:** 15 minutes on partnership opportunities?
>
> Hi Mr. Okafor,
>
> I'm Sam Reyes, head of partnerships at Brightline. We help logistics companies like yours cut last-mile delivery costs, and after reading about your expansion into the Midwest, I think there may be a strong fit.
>
> Could I grab 15 minutes next week to share a couple of specific ideas? I'm open Monday afternoon or Wednesday morning, whichever is easier for you. If now isn't the right time, I completely understand and can follow up later in the quarter.
>
> Best,
> Sam Reyes
> Brightline Partnerships

The graceful exit at the end ("if now isn't the right time") is intentional. Giving a busy person an easy way to decline often makes them more willing to engage, and it keeps the door open for the future.

## Common mistakes that quietly kill your response rate

Even well-meaning requests stumble on small things. Watch for these:

- **Burying the ask.** If the reader has to hunt for what you want, they may not bother. Keep the request near the end and make it visually distinct.
- **Being vague about purpose.** "I'd love to connect" tells them nothing. Name the topic.
- **Over-explaining.** A meeting request is not the place for your full pitch. Give just enough to earn the meeting, then save the rest for the meeting itself.
- **No duration.** Open-ended time commitments feel risky. Always say how long.
- **Forgetting your signature.** A clear sign-off with your name, role, and contact details builds trust, especially in cold outreach. If yours is missing or messy, a clean [email signature best practices](/email-signature-best-practices) guide can help you fix it.

## How and when to follow up

Silence is not always a no. People get busy, emails get buried, and a polite nudge often gets the response your first message did not. Wait two to three business days, then send a short, friendly follow-up in the same thread so the original context travels with it.

> Hi Dana,
>
> Just floating this back to the top of your inbox in case it slipped through. Still happy to walk you through the proposal whenever works for you. Would early next week be any easier?
>
> Thanks,
> Maria

Keep follow-ups light and brief. One nudge is almost always appropriate. A second, a week or so later, is fine. Beyond that, it is usually kinder to both of you to step back and try a different channel or a different time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a meeting request email be?

Short. Aim for three to five sentences in the body plus your sign-off. The reader should be able to understand who you are, what you want, how long it will take, and when, in well under a minute. If your draft runs long, you are probably explaining things that belong in the meeting itself. Trim until only the essentials remain.

### Should I suggest specific times or let them choose?

Suggest specific times. Offering two or three concrete windows is far more effective than asking "When are you free?" because it removes the mental work of checking a calendar and proposing options. Always include a graceful fallback ("if none of these work, send me a couple of times that do") so you are not boxing the reader in.

### What should I do if I get no response?

Send one friendly follow-up after two or three business days, replying within the original thread so the context stays attached. Keep it brief and assume good intent, since busy inboxes swallow plenty of legitimate requests. If a second nudge a week later also goes unanswered, take it as a soft no and either pause or reach out through a different channel.

## Putting it together

A strong meeting request is mostly about removing friction. State your purpose clearly, show the value, name a duration, offer specific times, and give the reader an easy way to respond. Do that consistently and your acceptance rate will climb, whether you are pitching a client, scheduling a one-on-one, or asking a stranger for fifteen minutes.
