Leaving a job is rarely simple, but the email that makes it official should be. A resignation email creates a clear record, gives your employer a defined last day, and sets the tone for everything that happens between now and your departure. This guide walks you through what to include, how to phrase it, and gives you copy-and-paste templates for different situations, plus advice on the parts people usually get wrong.

## What a Resignation Email Is For

A resignation email does three practical jobs at once:

- It formally notifies your employer that you are leaving.
- It states your last working day, which starts the clock on your notice period.
- It creates a dated, written record that protects both you and the company.

That third point matters more than most people realize. Verbal resignations get misremembered, and "when exactly did you say you were leaving?" is not a conversation you want six weeks from now. A short, dated email removes the ambiguity.

Keep your goals modest. This is not the place to relitigate grievances, negotiate a raise, or write a memoir of your time at the company. The best resignation emails are short, gracious, and unmistakably clear about the date.

## When to Send It (and Who to Tell First)

Order of operations is its own small etiquette. In most workplaces, you tell your direct manager before anyone else, ideally in a live conversation (in person, by phone, or by video), and then follow up with the written email. The email confirms what you said out loud.

A few timing rules of thumb:

1. **Check your contract or handbook for the required notice period.** Two weeks is a common default in many places, but some roles or contracts require more.
2. **Tell your manager first.** Finding out from a forwarded email or the grapevine stings, and it can sour an otherwise clean exit.
3. **Send the email early in the day, early in the week if you can.** It gives people time to respond and start planning while you are still around.
4. **Avoid Friday afternoons.** Your news will sit unread over the weekend and land cold on Monday.

## The Core Structure of a Resignation Email

Almost every good resignation email follows the same skeleton. You can adjust the warmth and length, but keep these pieces:

- **A clear subject line.** Name the document for what it is.
- **A direct statement of resignation.** Say you are resigning in the first sentence or two.
- **Your last working day.** Be specific with the date, not just "in two weeks."
- **A brief offer to help with the transition.** This is the part that earns goodwill.
- **A short note of thanks.** One or two genuine sentences, not a flood.
- **A professional sign-off.** Your name, and your contact details if a personal email matters.

You do not need to explain why you are leaving. "I have accepted another opportunity" or simply "I have decided to move on" is plenty. The reason is yours to share or keep.

### Subject lines that work

A vague subject line buries the most important email you will send your manager all year. Keep it plain:

> Subject: Resignation - Priya Desai

> Subject: Notice of Resignation - Effective June 20

> Subject: Resignation and Last Day

## Template 1: The Standard Two-Week Notice

This is the version most people need. It is warm, brief, and complete.

> Subject: Resignation - Marcus Lee
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> I am writing to formally resign from my position as Marketing Coordinator at Brightline Media. My last working day will be Friday, June 20.
>
> Thank you for the support and opportunities I have had over the past three years. I have learned a great deal here, and I am grateful for the chance to have worked with this team.
>
> Over the next two weeks, I am happy to help wrap up my current projects and document my work so the handover is smooth. Please let me know how I can make the transition as easy as possible.
>
> Best regards,
> Marcus Lee
> marcus.lee@email.com | (555) 014-2200

Notice what it does not do: it does not name the new employer, list complaints, or over-explain. The transition offer is concrete ("document my work") rather than a hollow "let me know if you need anything."

## Template 2: Leaving on Short Notice

Sometimes circumstances do not allow a full notice period. Acknowledge it honestly, apologize briefly, and stay constructive.

> Subject: Resignation - Effective Immediately
>
> Hi Robert,
>
> I am resigning from my role as Operations Assistant, effective immediately. I am sorry for the short notice. Due to a family situation that requires my full attention, I am unable to continue in the position.
>
> I have left my current task list and account passwords with the team lead, and I am reachable by email this week if anything urgent comes up.
>
> Thank you for your understanding, and for the experience I gained on the team.
>
> Sincerely,
> Anna Foster

One honest sentence about the reason ("a family situation") is enough. You are not obligated to share private details, and most managers will not push.

## Template 3: A Warm, Longer Goodbye

If you genuinely loved the job and want a fuller farewell, you can add a little more. Keep it sincere rather than sentimental.

> Subject: Resignation - Thank You
>
> Dear Maria,
>
> After a lot of thought, I have decided to resign from my position as Senior Designer at Nordic Studio. My last day will be July 2.
>
> This was not an easy decision. The past five years here shaped how I work and how I think about design, and I owe much of that to your mentorship and to a team I have been proud to be part of.
>
> I want to leave things in good shape. I will finish the client deliverables currently on my plate, write up handover notes for ongoing accounts, and help train whoever steps into the role. I am glad to stay flexible on the timing if that helps.
>
> Thank you for everything. I hope we stay in touch.
>
> Warm regards,
> Tom Becker

## What to Leave Out

A few things quietly damage an otherwise good resignation:

- **Negativity about people or the company.** Even if it is justified, the email lives forever and can be forwarded. Save honest feedback for an exit interview if you choose to give it.
- **Sarcasm or passive-aggressive thanks.** "Thanks for all the late nights" reads exactly how you think it does.
- **Demands or ultimatums.** If you want to negotiate a counteroffer, that is a separate, in-person conversation, not a line in your resignation.
- **Oversharing about the new job.** Salary, title, and the new company name are nobody's business at this stage.
- **A last day that is unclear.** "Sometime in the next few weeks" creates exactly the confusion this email is meant to prevent.

## Before You Hit Send

Run through this quick checklist:

1. Have I told my manager directly, before sending this?
2. Is my last working day a specific date?
3. Does the notice period match my contract?
4. Is the tone calm and gracious, even on a bad day?
5. Did I include a personal email or phone number if I want to stay reachable after my work account is disabled?
6. Did I reread it once more for typos and the right recipient?

That last point is not a joke. Sending your resignation to the wrong "Dana" in the directory is a memorable way to start your final two weeks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it acceptable to resign by email instead of in person?

Whenever possible, tell your manager first in a live conversation, then send the email as written confirmation. Email-only resignations are sometimes unavoidable (remote roles, an unreachable manager, an urgent personal situation), and they are perfectly valid. The email is the official record either way, so it should exist regardless of whether you also speak in person.

### How much notice should I give?

Start with whatever your contract or employee handbook specifies, since that is the binding number. Where nothing is stated, two weeks is a widely understood default for many roles, though senior or specialized positions often warrant more. Giving fair notice protects your professional reputation, and many people cross paths with former colleagues again later in their careers.

### Do I have to explain why I am leaving?

No. A simple line such as "I have accepted another opportunity" or "I have decided to move in a new direction" is entirely sufficient. You can share more if the relationship is warm and you want to, but you are never required to justify the decision in your resignation email. Detailed feedback, if you want to give it, belongs in an exit interview.

## A Calm Exit Is Worth the Effort

The job market is smaller than it feels, and the way you leave tends to outlast the reasons you left. A short, clear, gracious resignation email costs you ten minutes and buys you references, goodwill, and a clean conscience. Pick the template that fits your situation, swap in your real details, double-check the date and the recipient, and send it with confidence.
