An offer just landed in your inbox, and your stomach is doing something complicated. You want more, but the words "I want more" feel impossible to say without sounding greedy or risking the offer. Here is the good news: email is the best place to negotiate, because it gives you time to choose every word carefully. This guide walks you through countering an offer professionally, with a full example you can adapt to your own situation.

## Why negotiating by email works in your favor

In a live phone call, you have seconds to react, and most people fold under that pressure. Email removes the pressure entirely. You can draft, walk away, reread, and send only when the words are exactly right.

Email also creates a written record. The number you agree on, the start date, the signing bonus, all of it is documented in a thread you can refer back to. That protects both sides and prevents "I thought we said" misunderstandings later.

And almost nobody has their offer rescinded for a polite, well-reasoned counter. Employers expect negotiation. A respectful counter signals that you know your value, which is a quality they want in the person they just chose to hire.

## Do your research before you reply

You cannot anchor on a number you have not justified. Before you write a single word, gather your evidence.

- **Market data.** Look up the role, your location, and your experience level on salary sites and in any comp data shared by people in your network.
- **Your leverage.** A competing offer, a current salary, in-demand skills, or a specialist background all strengthen your position.
- **The full picture.** Find out what the company typically offers beyond base pay so you know what is negotiable.

Walk in knowing two numbers: the figure you will ask for (your anchor) and the figure you will accept (your floor). Your anchor should be ambitious but defensible, usually somewhat above what you actually expect to land on.

## Express enthusiasm before you counter

This is the single most important move, and the one people skip. Before you mention money, make it unmistakable that you want the job. Negotiation feels adversarial only when the employer is unsure whether you are leaving. Reassure them first.

A simple opening line does the work: "I am genuinely excited about this role and the team, and I want to make this work." Now the rest of your email reads as someone trying to say yes, not someone hunting for an exit.

If you have already built rapport through the process, lean on it. The same warm, specific tone that worked in your [interview follow-up email](/blog/interview-follow-up-email) belongs here too.

## Anchor with a specific number and a reason

Vague asks get vague answers. "Is there any flexibility?" invites a "no." A specific, justified number invites a real conversation.

State the figure clearly, then back it with one or two reasons. Good reasons include market data for the role, the scope of responsibilities, your specialized experience, or a competing offer. Avoid reasons rooted in your personal needs, like rent or student loans. Those are real, but they do not speak to your value to the company.

A clean ask looks like this: "Based on my research for this role and my [X] years leading [specific work], I was hoping we could land closer to [number]." Confident, specific, and grounded.

## Negotiate the whole package, not just base

Base salary is the headline, but it is not the only lever, and sometimes it is the least flexible one. If the company cannot move much on base, it may have room elsewhere.

- **Signing bonus.** Often easier to approve than a permanent base increase.
- **Equity or stock.** Especially relevant at startups and tech firms.
- **Start date, PTO, and remote flexibility.** Real value that does not hit the salary line.
- **A review timeline.** A scheduled compensation review at six months can bridge a gap now.
- **Title.** Sometimes a better title is worth more to your long-term earnings than a few thousand dollars today.

Rank what matters most to you in advance. If base is immovable, you want to know your next priority before they ask.

## Stay collaborative, not adversarial

The tone that wins is "we are solving this together," not "me versus you." Frame your counter as a shared problem you both want to close, and use language that keeps the door open.

- Say "Could we explore..." rather than "I need..."
- Say "I want to make this work" rather than "Otherwise I will have to decline."
- Ask questions instead of issuing ultimatums.

Real ultimatums should be rare and true. Never bluff about a competing offer, because if they call it, you have nothing left and you have damaged your credibility on day zero.

## A full counter-offer example

Here is a complete email putting it all together. Notice how it opens with enthusiasm, states a specific number with a reason, broadens to the full package, and closes collaboratively.

> **Subject:** Re: Offer for the Senior Analyst role
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> Thank you for the offer. I am genuinely excited about the role and the chance to work with your team, and I want to find a way to make this work.
>
> Before I sign, I would love to discuss the compensation. Based on my research for senior analyst roles in this market and the five years I spent building forecasting models at my current company, I was hoping we could bring the base closer to $112,000.
>
> If there is not full flexibility on base, I am open to creative solutions. A signing bonus, an earlier compensation review, or some additional PTO would all help close the gap.
>
> I am confident we can land somewhere that works for both of us. Happy to hop on a quick call if that is easier.
>
> Best,
> Jordan

That email is firm and specific without a hint of hostility. It gives the employer several paths to yes and makes clear that yes is the goal.

## How to handle a no gracefully

Sometimes the answer is "this is our best offer." How you respond shapes your first months on the job.

If you are taking it anyway, accept warmly and, if it fits, plant a seed: "Understood, and I am glad to move forward. Could we put a compensation review at the six-month mark on the calendar?" That keeps the door open without souring the start.

If the number genuinely does not work, decline with grace and gratitude. Burning a bridge over a few thousand dollars is rarely worth it, and the recruiter may remember you for the next, better-paying role. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it okay to negotiate over email instead of by phone?

Yes, and many people prefer it. Email gives you time to choose your words, gather your evidence, and avoid blurting out a number under pressure. It also creates a clear written record of what was agreed. Some recruiters may want to "hop on a call" to discuss, which is fine, just send your reasoning in writing first so your anchor is on the record before the conversation.

### How much higher than the offer should I counter?

A common approach is to counter somewhere between 5 and 15 percent above the offered base, depending on how far the offer sits below your market research. Anchor high enough to leave room for a meet-in-the-middle outcome, but keep it defensible with data. An ask that is wildly above market with no justification reads as uninformed and weakens your credibility.

### What if they ask for my salary expectations before making an offer?

Where it is legal to ask, try to deflect to a range based on your market research rather than naming a single hard number, and frame it around the value of the role. Something like "Based on the scope of this role and the market, I am targeting the $105,000 to $115,000 range" keeps you anchored without boxing yourself in. In many places employers are now required to share the posted range, so it is worth checking what the listing already discloses.

## The takeaway

Negotiating salary by email is not pushy, it is professional, and the format is your friend. Lead with enthusiasm, anchor on a specific number with a real reason, negotiate the whole package, and keep the tone collaborative throughout. Take the time email gives you to get every word right, and you will counter with confidence instead of dread.
