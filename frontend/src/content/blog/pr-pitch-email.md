A working journalist can receive hundreds of pitches in a single day, and most get deleted in the first two seconds. The ones that earn a reply are not the slickest. They are the most relevant, the most useful, and the most respectful of the reporter's time. This guide covers how to find the right journalist, lead with the story instead of your company, build a real news hook, and make a reporter's job so easy that covering you becomes the path of least resistance.

## Research the right reporter and beat

The fastest way to get deleted is to pitch the wrong person. A tech reporter does not want your restaurant opening, and a national editor does not cover your local 5K. Before you write a word, do the homework.

- **Read their recent work.** Look at the last several pieces they published. What do they actually cover, and how?
- **Match the beat, not just the outlet.** A publication has dozens of reporters with narrow specialties. Find the one whose beat your story fits.
- **Check their preferences.** Many journalists state in their bio how they want to be pitched. Honor it.
- **Pitch one reporter per outlet.** Blasting five people at the same publication is a fast way to be ignored by all of them.

A pitch sent to one well-chosen reporter beats a blast to fifty wrong ones every single time. This is the same relevance-over-volume principle behind effective [cold email outreach](/blog/cold-email-outreach).

## Personalize the first line

Reporters can spot a mail merge instantly, and a generic "Hi {name}, I hope you're well" signals that you did not read their work. The opening line should prove this pitch was meant for them.

- **Reference a specific recent piece.** "Your piece last week on small-business lending got me thinking about a story you might find useful."
- **Connect their beat to your angle.** Show you understand what they cover and why your story fits.
- **Keep the flattery honest and brief.** A short, specific nod beats gushing praise that sounds automated.

Personalization is not about charming the reporter. It is about demonstrating, in one line, that you respect their time enough to have done your homework.

## Lead with the story, not your company

This is the mistake that sinks most pitches. PR people lead with "We are excited to announce that our company has launched..." Reporters do not cover your excitement. They cover stories their readers care about.

Flip the frame. Open with the story angle and let your company be the supporting detail.

Weak pitch opener:

> I am reaching out on behalf of Brightline Software, a leading provider of inventory management solutions, to announce our new product release.

Stronger pitch opener:

> Small retailers are quietly losing thousands of dollars a year to inventory they forgot they had. We dug into the data from 2,000 independent shops and found a pattern worth a story.

The second version gives the reporter something to write about. Your product becomes the reason you have the data, not the headline.

## Build a real news hook

Reporters write about things that matter now. Without a reason this is timely, your pitch is just an ad. A news hook (sometimes called a news peg) answers the question "why should I write this this week?"

Common, legitimate hooks:

- **A timely tie-in.** Your story connects to a current event, season, or trend reporters are already covering.
- **Original data.** A survey, a study, or numbers no one else has. Journalists love exclusive data.
- **A genuine first or milestone.** A real first-of-its-kind, not a stretch.
- **A human story.** A specific person whose experience illustrates a larger trend.

If you cannot find a real reason it matters now, the story may not be ready to pitch yet. Forcing a fake hook reads as desperate and trains the reporter to ignore your next email.

## Keep it short and skimmable

Reporters scan on deadline. A long, dense pitch is a wall they will not climb. Respect that with brevity and structure.

- **Get to the point in the first two sentences.** Assume they will not read past them unless you earn it.
- **Use short paragraphs.** Two or three sentences each.
- **Bold or bullet the key facts** so the angle is graspable in a glance.
- **Aim for a screen, not a scroll.** If it does not fit without scrolling, it is too long.

A tight subject line carries a lot of weight here too. It should preview the story, not your brand. Our guide on [email subject lines](/blog/email-subject-lines) applies directly, since the same scan-in-a-second dynamic governs a reporter's inbox.

## Make their job easy

The pitches that get covered are the ones that hand the reporter most of what they need. Every bit of friction you remove raises your odds.

- **Offer the key facts up front.** The who, what, why, and why now.
- **Include a quotable line.** A ready-made quote from a named, relevant person.
- **Provide access.** Offer an interview, a demo, or a source the reporter can talk to.
- **Have assets ready.** Mention that images, data, or a one-pager are available, and send them promptly if asked.

Here is a strong, complete example pitch:

> Subject: Data: indie retailers lose $4,000 a year to "lost" inventory
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> Your piece last week on the squeeze facing small shops was sharp, and I have data that adds a surprising angle.
>
> We analyzed inventory records from 2,000 independent U.S. retailers and found the average store writes off about $4,000 a year in stock it literally forgot it had, often in back rooms and second locations. The smallest shops are hit hardest.
>
> A couple of findings that might interest your readers:
>
> Stores under five employees lose nearly twice the share of revenue that larger shops do. The problem spikes after the holidays, when overstock gets buried.
>
> I can share the full dataset, connect you with two shop owners who turned this around, and get you a quote from our lead analyst by tomorrow. Would any of that be useful for a piece?
>
> Thanks for your time,
> Jordan Lee
> Brightline Software

Notice it opens with the reporter's work, leads with data, offers sources and assets, and makes a low-pressure ask.

## Time it well and follow up with care

When you send matters, and how you follow up matters more. Reporters work on deadlines, and a pitch that lands at the wrong moment dies regardless of quality.

- **Send mid-morning on a weekday** as a safe default, and avoid Friday afternoons and the moments right before known deadlines.
- **Follow up once, briefly, after a few days.** Add something new (a fresh data point, an updated angle) rather than just "checking in."
- **Stop after one or two follow-ups.** Silence is an answer. Pestering burns the relationship for every future story.
- **Make it easy to decline.** A line like "no worries if this isn't a fit" keeps the door open.

The networking instincts that build long-term relationships matter here too, since coverage often comes from reporters who already trust you. Our guide on writing a [networking email](/blog/networking-email) covers building those connections before you need them.

## What gets pitches deleted

It helps to know the patterns that send a pitch straight to trash. Avoid these and you are already ahead of most senders.

- **Obvious mass blasts** with no personalization or wrong beat.
- **Leading with your company** instead of the story.
- **No news hook,** so there is no reason to write it now.
- **Walls of text** and attachments the reporter did not ask for.
- **Aggressive follow-ups** that demand a response.
- **Fake urgency or clickbait subject lines** that break trust on open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a media pitch be?

Short. A reporter should grasp the story, the hook, and what you are offering within a quick scan, which usually means under 150 words for the core pitch. Lead with the angle in the first two sentences, use a couple of short paragraphs or bullets for the key facts, and offer sources and assets rather than dumping everything in the email. You can always send more once they express interest, so the goal of the pitch is to earn that interest, not to tell the whole story.

### Should I attach a press release or images?

Not on the first email. Unrequested attachments can trip spam filters and signal a mass blast, and many reporters will not open them. Instead, mention that a one-pager, the full dataset, or high-resolution images are available, and send them promptly the moment the reporter asks. This keeps your initial email light and fast to read while showing you have the materials ready.

### How many times should I follow up?

Once, maybe twice, spaced a few days apart, and only if you can add something new each time. A single short follow-up that offers a fresh data point or a clearer angle is fair; repeated "just checking in" messages are not. If a reporter does not respond after two attempts, treat the silence as a polite no and move on. Pestering a journalist costs you not just this story but every future one.

## The takeaway

Earning coverage is not about volume or polish. It is about pitching the right reporter a real story, leading with their readers' interest instead of your company, and making the work of writing it about you as easy as possible. Do your homework, personalize the first line, build an honest hook, keep it short, and follow up with restraint. Get that right and you stop being one of the hundreds of deleted pitches and start being the email a journalist is glad they opened.
