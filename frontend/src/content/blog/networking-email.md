Most networking emails fail for the same reason: they ask the recipient to do work without giving them a reason to care. A good networking email respects the reader's time, makes the ask specific, and reads like it came from a person rather than a template. This guide walks through how to write one that actually gets a reply, with example emails you can adapt to your own situation.

## What makes a networking email different

A networking email is not a sales pitch and it is not a cold résumé. You are trying to start a relationship, or strengthen one, with another professional. That changes the rules.

The biggest mistake people make is treating outreach as a transaction. They lead with what they want (a job, an intro, advice, fifteen minutes on the calendar) before giving the reader any reason to invest. The reader has nothing to react to except the size of the favor.

Strong networking emails do three things instead:

- They give the reader a clear, honest reason for the contact ("we met at," "I read your piece on," "a mutual colleague suggested").
- They keep the ask small and specific enough to answer in a single reply.
- They make replying easy by removing decisions the reader would otherwise have to make.

Get those three right and the rest is polish.

## Start with a reason, not a request

The first two sentences decide whether someone keeps reading. Open with context that connects you to them, then state why you are reaching out. Save the ask for after you have established why they should care.

Compare these two openings:

> Hi Dana, I'm a marketing professional looking to grow my network and would love to connect and learn from your experience.

That tells the reader nothing about why *they* specifically are receiving this. It could go to anyone.

> Hi Dana, I heard your talk on retention loops at the Portland Product Meetup last week, and your point about onboarding emails doing more than the homepage stuck with me. I run lifecycle marketing at a small SaaS company and have been wrestling with the same problem.

The second version proves you paid attention and gives Dana a reason to recognize herself in the message. You have earned the next sentence.

If you have no direct connection, find the closest honest one: their published work, a shared employer, an alumni link, a person you both know. "Honest" matters. Recipients can tell when a "loved your article" line is generic, and a hollow compliment does more harm than no compliment at all.

## Make the ask small and specific

Vague asks create work. "Can I pick your brain?" forces the reader to figure out what you want, how long it will take, and whether it is worth it. Most people answer that question by not answering at all.

Specific asks are easy to say yes to. Instead of asking for an open-ended call, ask for one concrete thing:

- "Would you be open to a 15-minute call in the next couple of weeks? I have three specific questions about breaking into product management."
- "No need for a call. If you have a minute, I'd love to know which one resource helped you most when you were learning SQL."
- "Would you be willing to introduce me to someone on your design team? I can send a short blurb you can forward, so it takes you nothing to pass along."

Notice the last one does the reader's work for them. Offering to write the forwardable intro, propose times, or send questions in advance lowers the cost of saying yes. The easier you make the reply, the more replies you get.

## Keep it short and skimmable

Many people read email on a phone, between meetings, with half their attention. A long message reads as a chore. Aim for something that fits on one screen without scrolling, roughly 75 to 150 words.

A reliable structure:

1. **Hook** (one sentence): the connection or reason you are writing.
2. **Context** (one to two sentences): who you are and why it is relevant to them.
3. **The ask** (one sentence): one specific, small request.
4. **The easy out** (optional): permission to say no, or a way to make replying effortless.

Here is the whole thing assembled into a cold outreach email:

> **Subject:** Question from a fellow Portland product person
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> I caught your retention talk at the Portland Product Meetup and your line about onboarding emails outperforming the homepage has been rattling around my head since.
>
> I run lifecycle marketing at a small SaaS company (about 30 people), and I'm trying to rebuild our onboarding sequence from scratch. You've clearly done this at scale.
>
> Would you be open to a 15-minute call in the next two weeks? I have three specific questions and promise to keep it tight. If a call is too much, even a quick reply pointing me to a resource would mean a lot.
>
> Either way, thanks for the talk.
>
> Best,
> Marcus Lee
> Lifecycle Marketing, Northvane

That email gives a reason, proves attention, names a small ask, and offers a graceful exit. It takes under a minute to read and under a minute to answer.

## Write a subject line that earns the open

The subject line is the only thing many recipients see before deciding to open or ignore. Make it specific and human. Skip the words that scream marketing blast ("Quick question!!!", "Opportunity," "Let's connect").

Good subject lines tend to:

- Reference the shared context: "Met at the Portland Product Meetup"
- State the small ask plainly: "15 minutes about lifecycle marketing?"
- Name a mutual connection: "Priya Shah suggested I reach out"

A mutual name in the subject line is one of the strongest things you can use, because it borrows trust you have not yet built yourself. If someone offered to connect you, ask whether you can mention them by name.

## Follow up without being annoying

Silence is rarely a no. People are busy, your email got buried, the timing was wrong. A single, polite follow-up after about a week recovers a meaningful share of non-replies, and it costs you almost nothing.

Keep the follow-up short, reattach the original context, and add a small piece of new value or flexibility rather than just repeating yourself.

> **Subject:** Re: Met at the Portland Product Meetup
>
> Hi Dana, floating this back to the top of your inbox in case it slipped by. Totally understand if now is not a good time. If it helps, I'm happy to send my three questions over email so you can reply whenever suits you. Thanks again.

One follow-up is courteous. A second, weeks later, can be fine. Beyond that, let it go. Repeated pestering damages the relationship you were trying to build.

## Make it easy to reply, and easy to remember you

Small details signal that you are a real, credible professional. Use your real name. Write in plain language, not corporate filler. And give the reader a frictionless way to know who you are and how to reach you again.

A clean email signature does quiet work here. It tells the recipient your role, your company, and how to find you, without you having to spell it out in the body. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a networking email be?

Short. Aim for 75 to 150 words, or about one phone screen without scrolling. The goal is a message someone can read and respond to in under two minutes. If you find yourself explaining your entire background, you are writing a cover letter, not a networking email. Lead with the connection, state one small ask, and trust that details can come later once they have agreed to talk.

### What should I do if I get no response?

Send one polite follow-up after roughly a week, reattaching the original thread so they have context. Add a small piece of flexibility, like offering to send your questions over email instead of scheduling a call. If a second follow-up a couple of weeks later still gets nothing, move on gracefully. No reply is almost never personal, and preserving goodwill matters more than forcing a single conversation.

### Is it okay to ask for a job in a networking email?

Usually not in the first message. Leading with a job request turns a relationship into a transaction before any trust exists. Instead, ask for advice, perspective, or a specific introduction. If the conversation goes well, opportunities tend to surface naturally, often because the person offers rather than because you asked. People help those they have come to know and like, so build that first.

The best networking emails do not feel like networking at all. They feel like one person reaching out to another with genuine context, a reasonable request, and respect for the reader's time. Get those fundamentals right, send the follow-up when you need to, and let the relationships build from there.
