Most of your email is read on a phone, often standing in a line or between meetings, on a screen the size of a playing card. An email that looks fine on your laptop can be a wall of text, a truncated subject line, and an unclickable link on someone's phone. The good news is that mobile-friendly email is mostly just good email: shorter, clearer, and front-loaded. This guide shows you exactly how to write and format messages that are easy to read and act on from a small screen.

## Assume the Phone First

Here is the mindset shift: stop writing for the laptop you are typing on and start writing for the phone your reader is holding. A huge share of email is opened on mobile, and many of those messages are triaged in seconds. If your point is not visible at a glance, it gets swiped away to "deal with later," which often means never.

This does not mean dumbing things down. It means respecting the format. A phone screen shows far less at once, scrolling is the only way to see more, and a thumb is a clumsier pointer than a mouse. Every choice below comes back to those three facts.

## Front-Load the Point

On a small screen, the first sentence or two is most of what your reader sees before deciding whether to keep reading. Spend it well. State the point, the ask, or the headline up top, then add context below for anyone who wants it.

Compare these openings:

- **Buried:** "I hope you're doing well. I wanted to follow up on our conversation from last week regarding the budget review process, and after speaking with the finance team, I think there are a few things worth discussing before we move forward."
- **Front-loaded:** "Can you approve the revised budget by Thursday? Details below."

The second version gives a busy reader on a phone everything they need in one glance. This is the same discipline we cover in our guide to [writing concise emails](/blog/how-to-write-concise-emails), and it matters twice as much on mobile.

## Write Subject Lines That Do Not Get Cut Off

Mobile inboxes show far fewer characters of the subject line than desktop, often around 30 to 40 before it truncates. If your key word is at the end, the reader never sees it.

- **Put the most important word first.** "Invoice due Friday: action needed" beats "Following up on a few items including the invoice that is due."
- **Aim short.** Treat 30 to 40 characters as your working budget, not 60.
- **Skip the throat-clearing.** "Quick question about" wastes the prime real estate. Lead with the question's topic.

Our deeper guide to [email subject lines](/blog/email-subject-lines) breaks down what makes them open, and on mobile the front-loading rule is non-negotiable.

## Keep Paragraphs Short and Scannable

A paragraph that looks reasonable on a wide screen can fill an entire phone display, and a full screen of unbroken text reads as work. Break it up.

- Keep paragraphs to two or three sentences.
- Put white space between them. Air makes a message feel quick to read.
- Use a short bulleted list when you have three or more items, like this one.
- Bold the single most important line if there is one, so a skimmer catches it.

The goal is that someone can understand your email by skimming it, and read it fully only if they choose to. On a phone, skimmability is not laziness on their part. It is how mobile reading works.

## One Clear Action, Made Tappable

Decide what you want the reader to do and make it the obvious next step. Multiple asks scattered through a long message get lost on mobile, and the reader ends up doing none of them.

- **State one primary action** and put it on its own line.
- **Make links and buttons easy to tap.** A thumb needs a target it can hit without zooming. Avoid tiny links crammed next to each other, since a fat finger will miss or hit the wrong one.
- **Use descriptive link text,** not a raw URL. "View the proposal" is tappable and clear; a long pasted link wraps awkwardly and looks broken on a phone.
- **If you use a button in an HTML email,** give it generous padding so the whole shape is tappable, not just the words.

## Avoid the Things That Break on Small Screens

A few formatting choices look fine on desktop and fall apart on mobile. Skip them.

- **Wide tables and multi-column layouts.** They force horizontal scrolling or shrink to an unreadable size. If you must share tabular data, keep it to two columns or attach it instead.
- **Tiny fonts.** Anything under about 14 to 16 pixels is a strain on a phone. Bigger is safer.
- **Huge images that push the message down.** A reader should see your words, not scroll past a banner to find them.
- **Fixed-width designs.** Content that does not reflow to the screen width will be cut off or zoomed out.

How your email handles light and dark backgrounds also changes on mobile, where dark mode is extremely common. Our guide to [why emails break in dark mode](/blog/email-in-dark-mode) covers the logo and contrast fixes that matter most on a phone.

## Keep Your Signature Compact

Signatures are a frequent mobile offender. A signature loaded with a large logo, social icons, a banner, and five lines of contact detail can be longer than the actual message on a small screen, and it looks especially cluttered on a reply chain.

- **Keep it to a few lines:** name, title, company, and one or two ways to reach you.
- **Use a small, transparent logo** rather than a big image block.
- **Use real text, not an image of text,** so it stays sharp and readable on any screen.
- **Drop the decorative extras** that add height without adding value.

## See a Bloated Email Become Mobile-Friendly

Here is a real-feeling example. First, the version that lands as a wall of text on a phone.

> Subject: Following up on a few different things from our meeting earlier this week that I wanted to circle back on
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> I hope you're having a great week so far. I wanted to follow up on our conversation from Tuesday's meeting because there were several items we discussed that I think are worth revisiting, and I also had a couple of new thoughts since then. First, regarding the launch timeline, I think we should consider whether the proposed dates still make sense given everything else on the calendar. Second, on the budget, I had some questions about the figures in the latest version of the spreadsheet that we looked at together. Third, I wanted to check whether you had a chance to speak with the design team about the assets we need. Let me know your thoughts on all of this whenever you get a chance.
>
> Best,
> Jordan

Now the mobile-friendly rewrite.

> Subject: Launch timeline: can we lock dates by Friday?
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> Two things from Tuesday:
>
> 1. Launch dates: can we confirm them by Friday? My only worry is the overlap with the conference.
> 2. Budget: I have a question on the travel line in the latest sheet. Quick call Thursday?
>
> The design assets can wait until next week, no rush there.
>
> Thanks, Priya.
>
> Jordan

The second version fits on one screen, leads with the urgent item, separates the asks, and tells Priya what is not urgent so she can prioritize. Same content, a fraction of the friction.

## Preview Before You Send

The simplest mobile habit is also the most overlooked: look at your email on a phone before sending anything important. Send a test to yourself, open it on your phone, and check three things.

1. Does the subject line show your key word before it cuts off?
2. Is the point visible without scrolling?
3. Are the links and any buttons easy to tap with a thumb?

If you send marketing or bulk email, use a preview tool that renders across devices. Either way, the thirty seconds it takes to glance at the mobile view catches the problems your laptop hides.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should an email be for mobile?

Shorter than you think. As a rule, aim to fit the core message and the ask within the first screen, roughly five to ten short lines, so a reader does not have to scroll to understand why you wrote. If you genuinely need more, front-load the point and put the detail below for those who want it. The test is whether someone could act on your email after reading only the top portion.

### Should I just send plain-text emails to avoid mobile problems?

Plain text sidesteps most layout issues and is a perfectly good choice for everyday business email, especially one-to-one messages. The mobile problems in this guide mostly come from heavy HTML, wide tables, and oversized images, none of which plain text has. Reserve designed HTML for cases that truly benefit from it, like newsletters, and keep even those simple and single-column. When in doubt, plain and short wins on a phone.

### Do mobile-friendly habits hurt my email on desktop?

Not at all, and that is the point. Front-loading the message, short paragraphs, clear single asks, and a compact signature read just as well on a laptop. Designing for the small screen produces email that is easier for everyone, on every device. You lose nothing on desktop by writing for mobile, and you gain a lot in the inboxes where most of your readers actually are.

## The Takeaway

Writing for mobile is not a separate skill. It is good email writing with the constraints made visible: less room, faster reading, and a thumb instead of a mouse. Front-load your point, keep subject lines and paragraphs short, give one clear and tappable action, trim your signature, and preview on a phone before you send. Do that and your email works wherever it is opened, which today is mostly in someone's hand.
