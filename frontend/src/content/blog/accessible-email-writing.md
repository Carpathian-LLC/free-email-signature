Some of the people reading your email cannot see it the way you do. They might use a screen reader that speaks the text aloud, need large fonts or high contrast, or process information differently. Accessible email is not a niche concern. It is how you make sure your message actually reaches everyone you sent it to, and almost everything that helps people with disabilities makes your email clearer for everyone. This guide gives you concrete, practical changes you can make today, no special tools required.

## Why Accessibility in Email Is Worth Your Attention

A meaningful portion of any audience has a disability that affects how they read: low vision, blindness, color blindness, dyslexia, or a cognitive condition that makes dense text hard to follow. When your email assumes everyone sees a crisp, full-color screen and reads quickly, you quietly shut some readers out.

There is also a plain self-interest case. The same habits that help a screen-reader user (clear structure, descriptive links, real text instead of images) also help the person skimming on a cracked phone screen in bright sun. Accessible email is just well-built email. You are not adding work for a few people. You are removing friction for all of them.

## Write Subject Lines and Openings That Carry Meaning

A screen reader announces the subject line first, and many readers triage their inbox by subject alone. A vague subject like "Update" or "Following up" forces everyone to open the message to learn anything. A specific subject does the work up front.

- **Say what the email is about.** "Invoice 2294 is overdue, please pay by Friday" tells the reader everything before they open it.
- **Front-load the key word,** since mobile and screen-reader users may only hear the first few words.
- **Open the body with the point,** not pleasantries. A reader using assistive tech should not have to wade through "I hope this finds you well" to reach the reason you wrote.

This overlaps directly with the clarity we push for in our guide to [writing concise emails](/blog/how-to-write-concise-emails). Concise and accessible are usually the same email.

## Structure the Message So It Can Be Navigated

People using screen readers often move through content by jumping between headings and lists rather than reading top to bottom. A single undifferentiated block of text gives them nothing to navigate. Structure helps them, and it helps every reader skim.

- **Use real headings** if your email is long enough to need them, rather than just bolding a line and hoping it reads as a heading.
- **Break content into short paragraphs,** two or three sentences each.
- **Use proper bulleted or numbered lists** for groups of items, so a screen reader announces them as a list with a count, which orients the listener.
- **Put one idea per paragraph.** Dense, multi-topic paragraphs are hard for everyone and especially for readers with cognitive load challenges.

## Write Link Text That Makes Sense on Its Own

This is the single most common accessibility mistake in email, and it is easy to fix. Screen-reader users often pull up a list of all the links in a message and hear them out of context. A page full of links that all say "click here" or "read more" is useless: there is no way to tell them apart.

Write link text that describes the destination.

- **Avoid:** "To see the report, click here."
- **Better:** "See the [quarterly sales report](#)."
- **Avoid:** pasting a long raw URL, which a screen reader reads character by character.
- **Better:** wrap the link in meaningful words.

The rule is simple: a reader should know where a link goes from its text alone, with no surrounding sentence. This also makes your email look cleaner and reads better on mobile.

## Add Alt Text to Every Image

If you include images, write alternative text for each one. Alt text is the description a screen reader speaks in place of an image, and it is also what a sighted reader sees when an image fails to load (which, in email, happens constantly because many clients block images by default).

- **Describe the image's purpose,** not just its appearance. If a chart shows sales doubling, the alt text can say "Bar chart showing sales doubled from spring to summer."
- **For purely decorative images,** mark them as decorative or give them empty alt text so a screen reader skips them rather than announcing a meaningless filename.
- **Never put important text inside an image.** If your offer, date, or call to action lives only in a graphic, a screen reader cannot read it and image-blocking readers never see it. Put that information in real text in the body.

That last point connects to a broader rule: real text beats text-in-images every time, for accessibility, for search, for dark mode, and for load speed.

## Use Color Carefully, and Never Rely on It Alone

Color is a useful signal, but a lot of people cannot perceive the distinction you are drawing with it. Two rules cover most cases.

First, keep contrast high. Light gray text on a white background may look elegant on your screen and be nearly invisible to someone with low vision. Dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark one, with a strong difference between them, is readable for far more people. This matters even more across light and dark modes, which we cover in our guide to [why emails break in dark mode](/blog/email-in-dark-mode).

Second, never use color as the only way to convey information. "The items in red are urgent" fails completely for a reader who cannot distinguish red. Pair color with something else: a word ("Urgent:"), a symbol, bold text, or position. The color can stay as a bonus signal, but the meaning must survive without it.

## Use Plain, Direct Language

Accessibility includes the words themselves. Dense jargon, long winding sentences, and unexplained acronyms raise the barrier for readers with cognitive disabilities, for non-native speakers, and honestly for everyone reading in a hurry.

- **Prefer shorter sentences** with one idea each.
- **Spell out an acronym the first time** you use it.
- **Cut filler and corporate vocabulary** in favor of plain words. "Use" instead of "utilize," "help" instead of "facilitate."
- **State the action you want clearly,** so no one has to infer it.

These are good manners for every reader, which is why they sit at the center of basic [email etiquette](/blog/email-etiquette-rules). Clear writing is inclusive writing.

## Make Your Signature Accessible Too

Signatures are a quiet accessibility problem because so many of them are built as a single image, with the name, title, and contact details baked into a graphic. A screen reader cannot read any of it, and if images are blocked, the recipient sees nothing at all where your contact information should be.

Build a signature that works for everyone:

- **Use real text** for your name, title, company, and contact details, not an image of text.
- **Give your logo meaningful alt text,** usually just your company name, or mark it decorative if the company name is already in the text.
- **Keep contrast strong** so the text is readable in both light and dark modes.
- **Keep it simple,** which helps screen-reader users and everyone reading on a small screen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my email is accessible?

Start with a few quick self-checks: read your link text out of context and see if it still makes sense, confirm every image has alt text, check that you are not using color as the only signal, and make sure no important information lives only inside an image. For a stronger test, turn on your device's built-in screen reader and listen to your own email. Hearing it read aloud reveals problems your eyes skip right over. These checks take minutes and catch the large majority of issues.

### Do I need special software to send accessible emails?

No. Almost everything in this guide is a writing and formatting habit, not a tool: descriptive link text, real headings and lists, alt text on images, strong contrast, plain language, and real text instead of images of text. Your normal email client already supports all of it. Specialized accessibility checkers exist and are useful for large marketing campaigns, but for everyday email, good habits do the job.

### Is accessible email only a concern for big companies or marketers?

Not at all. Anyone you email might use assistive technology or simply read in conditions where clarity matters, and you usually have no way of knowing who. A job applicant, a customer, a colleague, or a board member could all benefit. The habits here cost you nothing and make every message clearer, so there is no reason to reserve them for formal campaigns. Treat accessible writing as your default, not a special mode.

## The Bottom Line

Accessible email is not a separate, harder kind of email. It is clear subject lines, real structure, link text that stands on its own, alt text on images, strong contrast, color that is never the only signal, plain language, and real text including in your signature. Every one of those choices helps a reader using assistive technology and also helps the person skimming on a phone. Write for everyone and your message reaches everyone, which was the whole point of sending it.
