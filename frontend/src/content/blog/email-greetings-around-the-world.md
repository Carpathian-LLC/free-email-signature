The way you open an email says more than you might think. A greeting that feels warm and normal in New York can read as oddly casual in Tokyo, and a formality that signals respect in Germany can come across as stiff in Sydney. If you write to people in different countries, knowing how email greetings shift across cultures helps you start on the right foot and avoid small mistakes that quietly cost you goodwill. Here is a practical, country-by-country look at how professionals actually open their messages, plus the underlying rules that travel everywhere.

## Why email greetings vary so much by culture

Every business culture sits somewhere on a few sliding scales. Some lean formal and hierarchical, where titles and full names matter and rushing to first names looks presumptuous. Others lean casual and egalitarian, where using someone's title can feel cold or even sarcastic. Some cultures value warmth and small talk up front, while others want you to get to the point quickly out of respect for the reader's time.

Two questions sit underneath almost every greeting choice:

- **How formal should I be?** This depends on the country, the industry, your relationship, and seniority.
- **How direct should I be?** Some cultures expect a softer, more relational opening before any request.

When in doubt, start more formal than you think you need to be. It is much easier to relax over time than to recover from an opening that felt too familiar.

## A country-by-country guide to email greetings

### United States and Canada

North American business email is friendly and fairly informal. First names appear quickly, often from the first message. "Hi [First name]" is the workhorse opener for most professional contexts, with "Hello [First name]" a slightly more formal alternative.

- Casual but professional: "Hi Sarah,"
- A touch more formal (first contact, senior person): "Hello Ms. Chen,"
- Cold outreach or applications: "Dear Mr. Patel," is still safe

Americans often add a quick friendly line before the request, like "Hope you're doing well" or "Hope your week is off to a good start." It is light social lubricant, not deep small talk.

### United Kingdom and Ireland

British email reads as slightly more reserved than American email, though first names are common once contact is established. "Hi [First name]" works for colleagues, while "Dear [First name]" or "Dear Mr./Ms. [Surname]" suits first contact and formal situations. A classic British nicety is "I hope this email finds you well."

A genuinely useful detail: in British style, the sign-off depends on the greeting. "Dear Mr. Smith" (named recipient) traditionally pairs with "Yours sincerely," while "Dear Sir or Madam" (unnamed) pairs with "Yours faithfully."

### Germany, Austria, and Switzerland

German-speaking business culture is more formal, and titles carry real weight. The standard formal opener is "Sehr geehrte Frau [Surname]" or "Sehr geehrter Herr [Surname]," roughly "Dear Mrs./Mr. [Surname]." Academic and professional titles like Dr. or Prof. are expected when someone holds them. People generally stay on last-name terms far longer than Americans do.

If you are writing in English to a German contact, keep it on the formal side at first:

> Dear Dr. Bauer,
>
> Thank you for taking the time to speak with me last week. As discussed, I am sending over the revised proposal for your review.

A friendlier German opener exists, "Hallo Herr Bauer," but let your counterpart set that tone before you adopt it.

### France, Spain, and Italy

French professional email tends to be formal and structured. The standard opener is simply "Bonjour Madame," or "Bonjour Monsieur," and in more formal writing "Madame," or "Monsieur," alone. First names are used less freely than in the US, and full titles are common in formal correspondence.

Spanish and Italian email is warm but still values courtesy. "Estimado/a [Name]" (Spanish) and "Gentile [Name]" (Italian) work for formal messages, while "Hola [Name]" and "Ciao [Name]" suit familiar contacts. Across these cultures, a brief courteous opening before the business matter is appreciated.

### Japan, South Korea, and China

East Asian business cultures place a high value on hierarchy, respect, and relationship. In Japanese, the honorific "-san" follows the surname (for example, "Tanaka-san"), and you typically lead with a polite seasonal or relational phrase before any request. Even in English, many Japanese professionals open with a softer, appreciative line.

> Dear Mr. Tanaka,
>
> Thank you very much for your continued support. I hope this message finds you and your team well.
>
> I am writing to follow up on the delivery schedule we discussed.

A few practical pointers when writing across these cultures:

- Use the surname plus title, and do not jump to first names unless invited.
- Lead with appreciation or a relational note before the ask.
- Keep requests polite and slightly indirect rather than blunt.
- In Chinese and Korean contexts, professional titles and seniority matter, so address the most senior person with appropriate respect.

### Nordic countries and the Netherlands

Scandinavia and the Netherlands are famously egalitarian and direct. First names are normal, formality is light, and people get to the point quickly. A simple "Hi [First name]" or "Hello [First name]" is standard, and long flowery openers can feel like padding. Being concise here is a sign of respect, not coldness.

### Australia and New Zealand

Australian and New Zealand business email is relaxed and warm. First names are the default, and openers like "Hi [First name]" or even "Hey [First name]" among familiar contacts are common. Over-formality can read as stiff. A friendly "Hope you're well" fits naturally.

### India and Southeast Asia

Indian professional email often blends British formality with genuine warmth. "Dear [First name]" and "Dear Mr./Ms. [Surname]" are common, and respectful titles matter, especially with senior people. Across much of Southeast Asia, politeness and relationship come first, so a courteous, slightly formal opener is a safe starting point.

## Rules that travel everywhere

No guide can cover every situation, but a handful of habits work across borders.

1. **Match the formality of the last message.** If someone signs off as "Best, Maria," you can reasonably reply with "Hi Maria." Mirroring is the simplest etiquette rule there is.
2. **Spell the name correctly and get the order right.** In some cultures the family name comes first. If you are unsure which name is the surname, a quick check is worth it. Few things undermine a greeting faster than the wrong name.
3. **Use a comma after the greeting, then start your first line.** "Dear Ms. Okafor," reads cleanly on its own line.
4. **When you cannot find a name,** prefer "Hello," or "Dear [Team/Role]," over "To whom it may concern," which feels dated in most regions. "Dear Hiring Manager," is fine for applications.
5. **Avoid assumptions about gender and marital status.** When unsure, using the person's full name ("Dear Alex Rivera,") sidesteps the problem entirely.

It also pays to remember that the greeting is only the first impression. The rest of your email, including a clean, professional sign-off and signature, carries the same message of care. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it ever wrong to use a first name in a professional email?

It is rarely "wrong," but it can be too familiar in formal or hierarchical cultures, especially on first contact in places like Germany, Japan, or France. The safe move is to start with a title and surname, then switch to first names once the other person does or once they sign off informally. Mirroring their style is the cleanest signal to follow.

### How do I greet a group of people in one email?

For a small, familiar group, "Hi all," or "Hi team," works well in most English-speaking and casual cultures. For a more formal or mixed audience, "Hello everyone," or "Dear all," is a courteous choice. Avoid gendered group greetings like "Dear Sirs," which exclude people and feel outdated. If the group is senior or formal, addressing them collectively with respect is better than guessing individual names.

### What should I write when I do not know the recipient's name?

Try to find the name first, even a quick look at the company website or LinkedIn often turns it up, because a named greeting always lands better. If you genuinely cannot find it, use a role-based greeting like "Dear Hiring Manager," or a neutral "Hello," rather than "To whom it may concern," which reads as impersonal in most regions today.

## Putting it into practice

You do not need to memorize the customs of every country. Just hold two questions in mind, how formal and how direct your reader expects you to be, and lean a little more formal whenever you are unsure. Watch how people sign their replies and mirror them, and you will rarely go wrong. A thoughtful greeting and a clean, consistent sign-off tell every recipient, in any culture, that you took their message seriously.
