Most cold emails fail for the same reason: they are written for the sender, not the reader. They open with a long introduction about the sender's company, bury the ask, and ask for far more than a stranger is willing to give. A cold email that gets a reply does the opposite. It respects the reader's time, makes one clear request, and gives them an easy reason to say yes. This guide walks through exactly how to write one, with real examples you can adapt today.

## What "cold" actually means

A cold email goes to someone who does not know you and is not expecting to hear from you. That is a high bar. The person already has a full inbox and zero obligation to respond. Your job is not to impress them. Your job is to be relevant enough, quickly enough, that replying feels easier than ignoring you.

That reframing changes everything about how you write. Instead of asking "how do I sell myself?" you ask "what does this specific person get out of reading and replying?" Every choice below comes back to that question.

## Start with research, not a template

The single biggest difference between cold emails that work and ones that get deleted is whether the sender did their homework. A reader can tell within the first line whether the email was sent to one person or one thousand.

Before you write a word, spend five minutes finding one specific, true thing about the recipient:

- A recent project, launch, article, or talk they were part of
- A problem their company is publicly trying to solve
- A shared connection, school, hometown, or former employer
- Something specific about their role that connects to your reason for writing

You do not need all of these. You need one real detail that proves you are writing to *them*, not to a list.

## The structure that works

A strong cold email has five parts and rarely runs longer than 120 words. Keep it short enough to read on a phone without scrolling.

1. **A relevant opener.** One sentence that shows you know who they are.
2. **The reason you are writing.** Why them, why now.
3. **The value or hook.** What is in it for them, stated plainly.
4. **One clear, small ask.** A single yes-or-no question or a tiny next step.
5. **An easy out.** Make declining painless. People reply more when they do not feel trapped.

That last part is counterintuitive but powerful. Giving someone permission to say no lowers the pressure, and lower pressure gets more replies.

### Example: reaching out to a potential client

Here is a freelancer emailing a small business owner. Notice it is specific, short, and asks for almost nothing.

> **Subject:** Quick idea for your new booking page
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> I booked a haircut through Maple Salon's new site last week and noticed the confirmation email goes out as plain text with no branding. I redesign these for local salons and usually see fewer no-shows once the confirmation looks professional.
>
> Would it be useful if I sent over a one-page mockup of what yours could look like? No cost, no obligation. If it is not a fit, a quick "not now" is totally fine.
>
> Best,
> Sam

This works because Sam used the service, named a real problem, offered something concrete (a free mockup), and made saying no easy.

### Example: a job seeker reaching out to a hiring manager

Cold emails are not just for sales. Job seekers who email a hiring manager directly often stand out from the application pile.

> **Subject:** Frontend role — saw your team shipped the new dashboard
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> I saw the analytics dashboard your team launched in May and the load-time improvements are exactly the kind of work I enjoy. I am a frontend engineer with four years building React apps, and I applied for your open frontend role last week.
>
> I would love two minutes of your read: would it help if I sent a short Loom walking through a similar performance fix I shipped? Happy to just leave my application as-is if that is easier on your end.
>
> Thanks for the work you put out,
> Alex

Alex referenced a real launch, connected their skills to it, and offered a low-effort way to learn more rather than demanding a call.

## Write a subject line that earns the open

If the subject line fails, nothing else matters. The goal is not to be clever. The goal is to be specific and honest about what is inside.

Subject lines that tend to work:

- Specific and relevant: "Quick idea for your new booking page"
- Referencing a shared detail: "Fellow Ohio State alum + a quick question"
- Honest about the ask: "5 min favor about your hiring process"

Subject lines that tend to fail:

- Vague hype: "An opportunity you can't miss"
- Fake familiarity: "Re: our conversation" (when there was none)
- All caps or heavy punctuation: "URGENT!!! Read this now"

Keep it under about eight words so it does not get cut off on mobile.

## Make the ask small

The most common mistake is asking for too much from a stranger. "Can we hop on a 30-minute call this week?" is a big request for someone who has never heard of you. Shrink the ask until saying yes costs almost nothing.

Strong small asks include:

- "Is this worth a quick look, yes or no?"
- "Want me to send the one-pager?"
- "Are you the right person for this, or should I reach out to someone else?"

The last one is especially good. It gives the reader an easy, helpful action even if the answer is "not me." Often they will forward you to the right person, which is a warm introduction you could not have gotten otherwise.

## Get the details right

Small mechanics quietly decide whether you look credible.

- **Use their name and spell it correctly.** Nothing kills a cold email faster than "Hi {FirstName}" or a misspelled name.
- **Send from a real, professional address.** A clear name and address build trust before they read a word.
- **Proofread once out loud.** Reading aloud catches the awkward phrasing your eyes skip over.
- **Send at a sensible time.** Mid-morning on a weekday tends to land better than late Friday afternoon, though it varies by industry.
- **Close with a clean signature.** A simple, professional signature with your name, role, and a way to verify who you are signals that you are a real person, not a bot. ## Follow up once, then let it go

Most replies do not come from the first email. A single, polite follow-up after a few business days often does the work. Keep it shorter than the original and add a small piece of new value if you can.

> **Subject:** Re: Quick idea for your new booking page
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> Floating this back up in case it slipped past. I went ahead and sketched a rough version of the confirmation email — happy to send the mockup whenever, or to drop it if the timing is off.
>
> Best,
> Sam

One follow-up shows persistence. Three or four shows you are not listening. If two emails get no response, move on graciously. The door stays open for later, and your reputation stays intact.

## A quick checklist before you hit send

Run through this every time:

- Does the first line prove I researched this specific person?
- Is the email under 120 words?
- Is there exactly one clear ask?
- Is it easy for them to say no?
- Is the subject line honest and specific?
- Did I spell their name right?
- Does my signature make me look like a real, trustworthy person?

If you can answer yes to all seven, you are ahead of the vast majority of cold emails this person will receive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a cold email be?

Short. Aim for under 120 words, or roughly the amount someone can read on a phone screen without scrolling. The longer your email, the more reasons you give the reader to put it off. Say who you are, why you are writing, what is in it for them, and what you want, then stop.

### What is the best time to send a cold email?

There is no single magic hour, and it depends on your audience. As a general rule, mid-morning on a Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday tends to perform well because people are at their desks but past the early-morning inbox rush. Avoid late Friday and weekends for business outreach. The more important factor is relevance, not timing, so do not obsess over the clock.

### How many times should I follow up?

Once is usually enough, sent a few business days after the original. If you want, a second short follow-up a week later is acceptable. Beyond that, you risk annoying the recipient and damaging your reputation. If two well-written emails get no reply, the answer is almost always no for now, and it is best to move on politely.

Writing a cold email that gets a reply is less about persuasion and more about respect: respect for the reader's time, their inbox, and their right to say no. Do your research, keep it short, make one small ask, and present yourself as a real person worth replying to. Get those basics right and you will stand out from nearly every other stranger sliding into that inbox.
