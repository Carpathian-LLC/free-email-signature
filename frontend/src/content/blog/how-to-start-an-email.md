The first line of an email does more work than people give it credit for. It sets the tone, signals how well you know the reader, and quietly tells them whether you are worth a careful reply. This guide walks through how to start an email for the situations you actually run into, with greetings you can copy, plus the small mistakes that make a good message land wrong.

## Why the Opening Line Matters

People decide how to read an email in the first second or two. A greeting that fits the relationship makes the rest of your message feel considered. One that is too stiff, too casual, or just wrong (the misspelled name, the "Dear Sir/Madam" to someone whose name is in the address) creates friction before you have made your point.

A strong email opening does three things at once:

- **Names the right tone.** Formal, friendly, or somewhere in between.
- **Acknowledges the reader.** It greets a person, not a void.
- **Earns the next line.** A clean start buys you attention for your actual request.

You do not need to overthink every message. But knowing a handful of reliable openers means you never stall on the first line again.

## The Building Blocks of an Email Greeting

Most email openings have two parts: the salutation (the "hello" word) and the name. Sometimes a third part follows, a short bridge sentence before you get to business.

### Choosing a salutation

Here is a rough ladder from most formal to most casual:

1. **Dear [Name],** Formal. Job applications, first contact with a senior person, legal or official matters.
2. **Hello [Name],** Professional and warm. Safe for almost any work context.
3. **Hi [Name],** Friendly and standard. The default for most colleagues and ongoing threads.
4. **Hey [Name],** Casual. Fine for people you know well, risky with strangers.
5. **[Name],** Direct, no salutation. Reads as brisk or even cold to some readers, so use it with people who match that style.

When unsure, "Hi [Name]" or "Hello [Name]" rarely goes wrong. They are warm without being presumptuous.

### Getting the name right

This is the part people rush, and it is the part recipients notice most.

- Spell it exactly as they spell it. Copy and paste from their signature or profile if you can.
- Match the form they use. If they sign off as "Kate," do not write "Katherine."
- When you do not know the name, do the small bit of research. A quick look at a company's team page or LinkedIn often turns up the right person.

A correctly spelled name says "I paid attention," which is the whole game.

## Greetings for Every Situation

Below are openings for the contexts that come up most, with example lines you can adapt.

### Cold outreach to someone you do not know

Lead with their name and a reason you are writing to *them* specifically, not a mass-mailed feeling.

> Hi Priya,
>
> I read your post on switching your team to async standups and wanted to ask how you handled the time-zone overlap. I run a small distributed team and we are hitting the exact problem you described.

Avoid "Dear Sir or Madam" and "To whom it may concern" whenever a real name is findable. They signal that you did not look.

### Applying for a job

Address the hiring manager or recruiter by name if the posting includes one. If it does not, a role-based greeting is acceptable and still warmer than the old fallbacks.

> Dear Mr. Okafor,
>
> I am applying for the Content Strategist role posted on your careers page. I have spent the last four years building blogs for early-stage SaaS companies, and your focus on developer-facing content is exactly the work I want to do next.

If you genuinely cannot find a name:

> Hello Hiring Team,

That reads better than "To whom it may concern" and acknowledges that a group of real people will read it.

### Replying to a colleague or ongoing thread

Keep it light and consistent with the thread. If the conversation is already moving, you can sometimes skip the salutation entirely after the first few replies. Early in a relationship, though, a quick "Hi [Name]" keeps things friendly.

> Hi Marcus,
>
> Thanks for sending the draft over. I have two small notes and one bigger question about the timeline.

### Following up when you have had no reply

Stay warm and assume the best. People are busy, not rude.

> Hi Dana,
>
> Circling back on my note from last week in case it slipped down your inbox. No rush at all, just wanted to keep it on your radar.

### Emailing a group

Pick a greeting that fits the group's size and formality.

- Small, familiar team: **Hi all,** or **Hi team,**
- A wider or more formal audience: **Hello everyone,**
- A mix of clients and colleagues: **Hi all,** stays safe and inclusive.

Skip gendered group greetings like "Hi guys" in professional settings, since they do not land well with everyone reading.

### Reaching out to someone senior or important

Lean slightly more formal than you think you need to, then dial back in later messages if they set a casual tone.

> Dear Dr. Lin,
>
> Thank you for agreeing to review the proposal. I have attached the latest version and flagged the two sections where your input would help most.

### A warm or thank-you note

When the whole point is gratitude or goodwill, let the greeting carry some of that warmth.

> Hi Sofia,
>
> I have been meaning to write since the conference. Your session on pricing changed how I am thinking about our next launch, and I wanted to say thank you properly.

## The Bridge Line After the Greeting

The sentence right after your greeting is a quiet chance to be human before you get to the ask. A short, specific bridge beats a generic one.

- **Generic:** "I hope this email finds you well." (Harmless but forgettable.)
- **Better:** "I hope your week is off to a good start." (Slightly warmer.)
- **Best:** A specific reference. "Congrats on the launch last week, the new dashboard looks great."

You do not always need a bridge. For quick, transactional emails, going straight to the point is a kindness. Save the warm-up for messages where the relationship matters.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Defaulting to "Dear Sir or Madam"** when a name is one search away.
- **Over-apologizing in the opener** ("So sorry to bother you..."). It undercuts your message before it starts.
- **Mismatched tone.** "Hey!!!" to a hiring manager, or "Dear Mr. Patel," to a teammate you message daily.
- **Forgetting the comma.** "Hi Sarah" without a comma reads as unfinished to many people. "Hi Sarah," looks complete.
- **Starting with "I."** Leading every email with what you want can feel one-sided. A brief acknowledgment first softens the ask.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is "Dear" too formal for most emails?

For everyday work email, yes, "Dear" can feel stiff. It still fits job applications, first contact with senior people, and formal or official messages. For colleagues, clients you know, and ongoing threads, "Hi [Name]" or "Hello [Name]" strikes a friendlier, more natural note.

### What should I write when I do not know the recipient's name?

First, try to find it. A company team page, the email domain, or a quick search often surfaces the right person. If you truly cannot, use a role-based greeting such as "Hello Hiring Team," or "Hi there," rather than the dated "To whom it may concern," which signals you did not look.

### Do I really need a comma after the greeting?

It is a small thing, but yes, a comma (or in very formal cases a colon) makes the line look finished and polished. "Hi Jordan," reads cleanly, while "Hi Jordan" with no punctuation can look like you hit send too early.

## Wrapping Up

A good email greeting is mostly about fit: the right level of warmth, the right name spelled the right way, and a first line that respects the reader's time. Keep a few reliable openers in your back pocket, match them to the moment, and the rest of the message gets easier to write.

While you are tightening up your emails, it is worth giving the bottom of your messages the same care as the top.
